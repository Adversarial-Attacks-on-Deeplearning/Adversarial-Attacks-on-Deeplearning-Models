\part{Computer Vision}

Computer Vision, a field of artificial intelligence and computer science, strives to grant machines the ability to see, interpret, and understand the visual world. In a manner analogous to human vision, it processes and analyzes data from images and videos to produce numerical or symbolic information. In recent years, driven by the revolutionary success of deep learning, particularly \textbf{Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)}, computer vision has transitioned from a niche academic pursuit to a cornerstone of modern technology. Its applications are pervasive, powering everything from autonomous vehicles and facial recognition systems to medical image analysis and automated quality control in manufacturing.

Despite their often superhuman performance on specific tasks, the deep learning models that underpin these systems exhibit a critical and surprising vulnerability: a profound sensitivity to adversarial attacks. These attacks consist of meticulously crafted, often imperceptible perturbations to input data that are designed to cause the model to make a confident but incorrect prediction. For example, a minor, human-unnoticeable modification to the pixels of an image can cause a state-of-the-art classifier to misidentify a panda as a gibbon, or a stop sign as a speed limit sign.

The implications of this fragility are profound and far-reaching, posing significant security and safety risks. In safety-critical systems like autonomous driving, an adversarial attack that fools an object detector could have catastrophic consequences. Similarly, in medical diagnostics, a manipulated scan that leads to a misdiagnosis could endanger patient health. Therefore, understanding the mechanics of these attacks and developing robust defense mechanisms is not merely an academic exercise but a fundamental requirement for the trustworthy deployment of AI in the real world.

This part of the thesis embarks on a systematic investigation of adversarial phenomena across core computer vision tasks. We will dissect these vulnerabilities by examining three fundamental and progressively complex domains:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Image Classification:} The foundational task of assigning a single label to an entire image.
    \item \textbf{Image Segmentation:} A more granular task requiring a pixel-level classification to partition an image into meaningful segments.
    \item \textbf{Object Detection:} A composite task that combines classification with localization to identify and draw bounding boxes around multiple objects within a scene.
\end{enumerate}

By exploring these three pillars of computer vision, we aim to provide a comprehensive overview of the threat landscape. For each domain, our approach will be consistent: we will first introduce the task and the benchmark models and datasets under consideration, then explore prominent adversarial attack strategies tailored to that task, and finally, analyze state-of-the-art defense mechanisms designed to enhance model robustness.
\chapter{Image Classification}
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:classification_intro}

Image Classification is a cornerstone of computer vision, serving as the foundational task of assigning a single, predefined label to an entire image. From distinguishing between species of animals to identifying products in a warehouse, its goal is to provide a high-level understanding of an image's primary content. The methodologies developed for image classification are often the building blocks for more complex vision tasks, such as object detection and segmentation, making its study essential for understanding the broader field.

While general-purpose classification is a well-studied area, the stakes are significantly elevated in safety-critical domains. One such domain is autonomous driving, where the correct identification of road signs is non-negotiable. A model's failure to distinguish a `Stop` sign from a `Speed Limit 60` sign could have catastrophic consequences. To ground our investigation in this high-stakes context, our experiments in this chapter will focus on the \textbf{German Traffic Sign Recognition Benchmark (GTSRB)} dataset. This dataset is a real-world, multi-class benchmark that presents a realistic challenge for modern classifiers and serves as an ideal testbed for evaluating security vulnerabilities.

The model under adversarial investigation is \textbf{EfficientNet-B1}. Unlike traditional architectures that arbitrarily scale dimensions like network depth or width, the EfficientNet family of models employs a principled method of \textit{compound scaling}, which systematically and uniformly scales the network's depth, width, and resolution. This approach has allowed EfficientNet models to achieve state-of-the-art accuracy while being significantly more computationally efficient than their predecessors. The choice of EfficientNet-B1 is deliberate: its combination of high accuracy and efficiency makes it a practical and powerful model for real-world deployment, and therefore, a crucial target for robustness analysis. Our trained EfficientNet-B1 model achieves a baseline test accuracy of \textbf{96.7\%} on the clean GTSRB test set, establishing a high-performance benchmark from which to measure the impact of adversarial attacks. If such an advanced and efficient model can be easily fooled, it underscores the severity of the adversarial threat.

In this chapter, we will therefore probe the vulnerabilities of the highly capable EfficientNet-B1 model when trained on the safety-critical GTSRB dataset. The subsequent sections will detail the specific adversarial attacks employed to generate malicious inputs and evaluate various defense strategies aimed at fortifying the model against such threats.
\section{Attacks}
\subsection{White Box Attacks}
\subsubsection{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)}

\paragraph{Introduction}
The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) is one of the earliest and most influential approaches for crafting adversarial examples against deep neural networks. Introduced by Goodfellow et al.\ in 2015, FGSM demonstrated that small, carefully chosen perturbations—imperceptible to humans—can reliably force state-of-the-art classifiers into making incorrect predictions. This work highlighted a fundamental vulnerability in high-dimensional linear behavior of models and sparked a wealth of research into both stronger attacks and robust defense mechanisms \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}.  

In their seminal paper, Goodfellow and colleagues showed that even a single-step perturbation computed from the model’s loss gradient suffices to degrade accuracy dramatically on datasets such as MNIST and CIFAR-10. The simplicity and effectiveness of FGSM make it a canonical benchmark for evaluating adversarial robustness and for developing more advanced multi-step attacks and defenses \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}.
\paragraph{Methodology}
\subparagraph{Attack Type}
The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) is a \textbf{white-box}, \textbf{untargeted} attack. As a white-box method, it requires full access to the model’s parameters and gradients. It is untargeted in the sense that its goal is simply to cause misclassification, not to steer the model toward a specific incorrect label. The adversarial example is generated to increase the loss with respect to the true class, thereby pushing the prediction away from the correct category.

\subparagraph{Parameters}
\begin{description}
  \item[$x$] The clean input image (e.g., a batch of images) to be perturbed.
  \item[$y$] The true label (or labels) associated with $x$.
  \item[$\theta$] The fixed parameters of the target neural network model.
  \item[$\epsilon$] The maximum perturbation magnitude (controls attack strength).
  \item[$J(\theta, x, y)$] The loss function used during model training (e.g., cross‐entropy).
\end{description}

\subparagraph{Algorithm Overview}
FGSM generates an adversarial example by taking a single, maximally disruptive step in the direction that increases the model’s loss most rapidly. Concretely, we compute the gradient of the loss $J(\theta, x, y)$ with respect to the input $x$, extract its sign to form a unit‐step “direction,” scale that by $\epsilon$, and add it to the original image. Finally, we clip the result to the valid pixel range to ensure a legitimate image. This one‐step procedure is efficient and often sufficient to cause high‐confidence misclassifications, making it a standard baseline for evaluating robustness.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) Attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x$, $y$, $\theta$, $\epsilon$
\Ensure $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
\State Enable gradient tracking on $x$ 
\State Compute loss: $L \gets J(\theta, x, y)$
\State Compute gradient: $g \gets \nabla_x L$ via backpropagation
\State Form perturbation: $\delta \gets \epsilon \cdot \mathrm{sign}(g)$
\State Generate adversarial example: $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets x + \delta$
\State Clip to valid range: $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets \mathrm{clip}(x_{\mathrm{adv}}, 0, 1)$
\State \Return $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsubsection{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)}
\paragraph{Introduction}

Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) is a powerful iterative attack method introduced by Madry et al.\ in their work on adversarial robustness \cite{madry2018towardspgd}. It is widely regarded as one of the most effective first-order adversarial attacks, capable of producing stronger perturbations than single-step methods like FGSM by applying multiple small gradient steps.

PGD operates by iteratively perturbing the input in the direction of the gradient of the loss function and projecting the perturbed input back into an $\epsilon$-bounded $\ell_\infty$ ball around the original input. This ensures that the adversarial example remains within a specified perturbation budget. Due to its effectiveness and reliability, PGD is often considered the “universal first-order adversary” and is commonly used both as a benchmark attack and as a training strategy (i.e., adversarial training) to improve model robustness \cite{madry2018towardspgd}.

\paragraph{Methodology}

\subparagraph{Attack Type}
The Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attack is a \textbf{white-box}, \textbf{untargeted}, and \textbf{iterative} attack. As a white-box method, it requires full access to the model’s parameters and gradients. It is untargeted in that its objective is to cause misclassification of the input without steering the prediction toward any specific incorrect label. The attack refines the basic idea of FGSM by applying multiple small perturbation steps, with each step constrained within an $\ell_\infty$-norm ball of radius $\epsilon$ centered around the original input. After each step, the perturbation is projected back into this bounded space, ensuring the adversarial example stays within the allowed perturbation budget.

\subparagraph{Parameters}
\begin{description}
  \item[$x$] The clean input image to be perturbed.
  \item[$y$] The true label associated with $x$.
  \item[$\theta$] The parameters of the target neural network model.
  \item[$\epsilon$] The maximum allowable perturbation (controls the size of the $\ell_\infty$ ball).
  \item[$\alpha$] The step size used for each gradient update.
  \item[$n$] The number of iterations (steps) to apply the attack.
  \item[$J(\theta, x, y)$] The loss function used during training (e.g., cross-entropy).
\end{description}
\subparagraph{Algorithm Overview}
The PGD attack is an iterative extension of the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM). It starts by adding a small random initialization within the $\ell_\infty$ ball of radius $\epsilon$ around the original input $x$. Then, for a fixed number of iterations $n$, the method performs gradient ascent on the loss function with respect to the input, stepping in the direction that increases the loss. After each step, the adversarial example is projected back into the valid $\ell_\infty$ ball to ensure that the perturbation does not exceed the maximum allowed distortion $\epsilon$. The adversarial example is also clipped to stay within the valid input range (e.g., pixel values between 0 and 1). This iterative process allows PGD to produce much stronger adversarial examples than single-step methods like FGSM.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) Attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x$, $y$, $\theta$, $\epsilon$, $\alpha$, $n$
\Ensure $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
\State Initialize $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets x + \text{Uniform}(-\epsilon, \epsilon)$
\State Clip $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets \mathrm{clip}(x_{\mathrm{adv}}, 0, 1)$
\For{$i = 1$ to $n$}
    \State Enable gradient tracking on $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
    \State Compute loss: $L \gets J(\theta, x_{\mathrm{adv}}, y)$
    \State Compute gradient: $g \gets \nabla_{x_{\mathrm{adv}}} L$
    \State Update adversarial example: $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets x_{\mathrm{adv}} + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{sign}(g)$
    \State Project: $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets \mathrm{clip}(x_{\mathrm{adv}}, x - \epsilon, x + \epsilon)$
    \State Clip to valid range: $x_{\mathrm{adv}} \gets \mathrm{clip}(x_{\mathrm{adv}}, 0, 1)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\paragraph{Results}

To evaluate the effectiveness of the PGD attack, we used the same \textbf{EfficientNetB1} model trained on the \textbf{German Traffic Sign Recognition Benchmark (GTSRB)} dataset, which achieved \textbf{96\% accuracy} on the clean test set. For adversarial evaluation, we applied PGD with varying perturbation budgets $\epsilon$ and corresponding step sizes $\alpha$, using a random subset of \textbf{512 test images}.

Table~\ref{tab:pgd_results} shows how model performance degrades under the PGD attack. Compared to FGSM, PGD leads to significantly lower accuracies at similar or even slightly higher $\epsilon$ values, due to its iterative nature. At $\epsilon=0.01$, accuracy already drops to 35\%. At $\epsilon=0.1$, the attack is completely successful, driving the model’s accuracy down to 0\%.

\subsection{Black Box Attacks}
\subsubsection{Boundary Attack}

\paragraph{Introduction}
The Boundary Attack is a decision-based adversarial attack that operates in a black-box setting, requiring access only to the model's final predicted class label. First introduced by Brendel et al.\ in 2018 \cite{brendel2018decisionbasedadversarialattacksreliable}, it is particularly powerful because it avoids reliance on gradients or confidence scores, making it suitable for attacking systems where internal model information is completely inaccessible. At its core, the attack performs an iterative random walk along the model's decision boundary to find a minimal perturbation that causes a misclassification.

\paragraph{Methodology}

The Boundary Attack is a \textbf{black-box} and \textbf{decision-based} method. A crucial aspect of this attack is how the initial adversarial example is chosen, as this defines the attack's goal. There are two primary strategies:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Untargeted Attack:} An initial adversarial example is created, often by refining a pre-existing attack or adding noise. The goal is simply to minimize this perturbation while ensuring the model makes \textit{any} incorrect prediction.
    \item \textbf{Targeted Attack:} The attack is initialized with a clean image from a different, specific class (e.g., initializing with a "Speed limit 70" sign to attack a "Speed limit 30" sign). The goal is then to "morph" this starting image to be as visually similar to the original target image as possible, effectively forcing the model to misclassify the target as the starting class. This makes the second strategy a natural and effective way to perform a targeted attack.
\end{enumerate}

\subparagraph{Parameters}
\begin{description}
  \item[$x$] The original clean input that is correctly classified by the model.
  \item[$x_{\mathrm{adv}}$] The starting adversarial example, which must already be misclassified.
  \item[$\mathcal{M}$] The black-box model to be attacked (returns only predicted class labels).
  \item[$\text{max\_iterations}$] The maximum number of attack iterations.
  \item[$\epsilon$] The step size controlling the exploration during the random walk.
\end{description}




\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Boundary Attack}
\label{alg:boundary_attack_core}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x$, $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(0)}$, $\mathcal{M}$, $\epsilon$, $\delta$
\Ensure $x_{\mathrm{adv}}$
\State $i \gets 0$
\While{$\|x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i)} - x\| > \delta$ and $i < \text{max\_iterations}$}
    \State Sample a perturbation direction $r$ from a normal distribution
    \State Normalize and scale: $r \gets \epsilon \cdot \frac{r}{\|r\|}$
    \State Propose a candidate: $x_{\mathrm{cand}} \gets x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i)} + r$
    \State Project toward original input: $x_{\mathrm{cand}} \gets x + (x_{\mathrm{cand}} - x) \cdot \min\left(1, \frac{\|x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i)} - x\|}{\|x_{\mathrm{cand}} - x\|} \right)$
    \If{$\mathcal{M}(x_{\mathrm{cand}}) \ne \mathcal{M}(x)$}
        \State Accept: $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i+1)} \gets x_{\mathrm{cand}}$
    \Else
        \State Reject: $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i+1)} \gets x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i)}$
    \EndIf
    \State $i \gets i + 1$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $x_{\mathrm{adv}}^{(i)}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}
\paragraph{Experimental Results}
To evaluate the Boundary Attack on our trained model, we conducted experiments demonstrating both initialization strategies.

\subparagraph{Strategy 1: Refining an Existing Adversarial Example}
First, we demonstrate the attack's ability to minimize an existing perturbation. We began with a pre-crafted adversarial example—a noisy "Speed limit 20" sign that our model misclassifies as "Slippery road" with 100\% confidence. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:boundary_attack}, the Boundary Attack uses this image as its starting point. It then iteratively refines the image, significantly reducing the visual distortion while keeping it on the adversarial side of the decision boundary. The final boundary example is much cleaner, yet it remains misclassified, with the model's confidence dropping to 49.97\%. This shows the attack's effectiveness in taking a powerful but obvious attack and transforming it into a much stealthier one.

\subsubsection{HopSkipJumpAttack}

\paragraph{Introduction}

The HopSkipJumpAttack, introduced by Chen et al.\ in 2019 \cite{chen2020hopskipjumpattackqueryefficientdecisionbasedattack}, represents a significant advancement in the field of decision-based black-box attacks. It is specifically designed to be highly \textbf{query-efficient}, meaning it requires far fewer interactions with the target model to find an adversarial example compared to its predecessors, such as the standard Boundary Attack. This efficiency is a critical feature for evaluating real-world systems, which may be protected by query limits or other defenses that detect an unusually high volume of requests.

The core innovation of HopSkipJumpAttack is its novel method for estimating the model's gradient direction using only the binary decision outputs (i.e., "is this image class A or not?"). While the standard Boundary Attack relies on a less-directed random walk to explore the decision boundary, HopSkipJumpAttack uses its gradient estimate to guide the search much more effectively. This allows the attack to find high-quality adversarial examples with minimal, often imperceptible, perturbations in a fraction of the time and with significantly fewer model queries.
\paragraph{Methodology}

\subparagraph{Attack Type}
The HopSkipJumpAttack is a \textbf{black-box}, \textbf{decision-based} adversarial attack. This means it treats the target model as an opaque oracle, interacting with it solely by providing input images and observing the final predicted label. It does not require access to model gradients, architecture, or confidence scores (logits). The attack can be used for both \textbf{untargeted} objectives (causing any misclassification) and \textbf{targeted} objectives (causing a specific misclassification). Its primary strength lies in its query efficiency compared to other decision-based methods.

\subparagraph{Parameters}
The algorithm's execution is governed by several key parameters:
\begin{description}
  \item[$x_{orig}$] The original, clean input image that is correctly classified by the model.
  \item[$\mathcal{M}$] The black-box target model, which returns only a final class label for a given input.
  \item[$max\_iterations$] The total number of iterations for the main attack loop.
  \item[$max\_queries$] A hard limit on the total number of times the model can be queried to ensure efficiency.
  \item[$\delta$] The perturbation size used for the Monte Carlo gradient estimation.
  \item[$B$] The batch size, or number of random directions sampled, for estimating the gradient.
\end{description}

% --- PHASE 1 ---
\subparagraph{Phase 1: Adversarial Initialization}
The first step of any decision-based attack is to find a starting point that is already in the adversarial region. Since the attack starts with only the original clean image, this is typically achieved by adding progressively larger amounts of random noise to the original image until the target model misclassifies it. Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_init} outlines this process.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Adversarial Initialization}
\label{alg:hsja_init}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Original image $x_{orig}$, Target model $\mathcal{M}$
\Ensure An initial adversarial example $x_{adv}$
\State $y_{orig} \gets \mathcal{M}(x_{orig})$
\State $\alpha \gets \text{initial\_noise\_factor}$
\For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} max\_attempts}
    \State $\text{noise} \gets \text{Uniform}(-\alpha, \alpha)$
    \State $x_{cand} \gets \text{clip}(x_{orig} + \text{noise})$
    \If{$\mathcal{M}(x_{cand}) \neq y_{orig}$}
        \State \Return $x_{cand}$
    \EndIf
    \State $\alpha \gets \alpha \times \text{growth\_factor}$
\EndFor
\State \Return \textbf{null}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

% --- PHASE 2 ---
\subparagraph{Phase 2: Iterative Refinement}
Once an initial adversarial point is found, the main iterative refinement loop begins. The overall structure of this loop is shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_main}. It orchestrates the three core components of the attack—the "Hop," "Skip," and "Jump"—to progressively move the adversarial example closer to the original image. Each of these components is detailed in the subsequent algorithms.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{HopSkipJumpAttack (Main Loop)}
\label{alg:hsja_main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Original image $x_{orig}$, Target model $\mathcal{M}$
\Ensure A final, refined adversarial example $x_{final}$
\State $x_{adv} \gets \text{Adversarial-Initialization}(x_{orig}, \mathcal{M})$ \Comment{See Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_init}}
\If{$x_{adv}$ is \textbf{null}} \Return $x_{orig}$ \EndIf
\State $x_{best} \gets x_{adv}$
\For{$t \gets 1$ \textbf{to} max\_iterations}
    \If{query budget exceeded} \textbf{break} \EndIf
    \State $\text{grad\_est} \gets \text{Estimate-Gradient-Hop}(x_{adv}, \mathcal{M}, ...)$ \Comment{See Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_hop}}
    \State $x_{cand} \gets \text{Geometric-Step-Search-Skip}(x_{adv}, \text{grad\_est}, ...)$ \Comment{See Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_skip}}
    \State $x_{adv} \gets \text{Binary-Search-Projection-Jump}(x_{cand}, x_{orig}, \mathcal{M})$ \Comment{See Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_jump}}
    \If{$\|x_{adv} - x_{orig}\| < \|x_{best} - x_{orig}\|$}
        \State $x_{best} \gets x_{adv}$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $x_{best}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

% --- COMPONENT DETAILS ---
\subparagraph{Component 1: The "Hop" (Gradient Estimation)}
The first step within the main loop is to estimate the gradient direction at the current boundary point. As shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_hop}, this is done via a Monte Carlo method. Many random vectors are sampled, and for each, the model is queried to see if adding that small perturbation causes the image to cross the decision boundary. The average of these directional outcomes provides a robust estimate of the true gradient.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Gradient Estimation ("Hop")}
\label{alg:hsja_hop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Current adversarial point $x_{adv}$, Model $\mathcal{M}$, Perturbation size $\delta$, Batch size $B$
\Ensure Estimated gradient direction $\text{grad\_est}$
\State $y_{orig} \gets \mathcal{M}(x_{orig})$
\State $\text{grad\_est} \gets \text{zero\_vector}$
\For{$b \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $B$}
    \State Sample a random direction vector $u_b$ and normalize it: $u_b \gets u_b / \|u_b\|$
    \State $x_{cand} \gets x_{adv} + \delta \cdot u_b$
    \If{$\mathcal{M}(x_{cand}) \neq y_{orig}$}
        \State $\text{grad\_est} \gets \text{grad\_est} + u_b$
    \Else
        \State $\text{grad\_est} \gets \text{grad\_est} - u_b$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\text{grad\_est} / \|\text{grad\_est}\|$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subparagraph{Component 2: The "Skip" (Geometric Step Search)}
After estimating the gradient, the algorithm must take a step away from the boundary to allow room for the next "Jump." As detailed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_skip}, it performs a search with geometrically increasing step sizes along the gradient direction until it finds a point that is safely inside the adversarial region.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Geometric Step Search ("Skip")}
\label{alg:hsja_skip}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Current point $x_{adv}$, Gradient estimate $\text{grad\_est}$, Model $\mathcal{M}$
\Ensure A new adversarial point $x'_{adv}$ further from the boundary
\State $y_{orig} \gets \mathcal{M}(x_{orig})$
\State $\epsilon \gets \text{initial\_step\_size}$
\Loop
    \State $x'_{adv} \gets x_{adv} + \epsilon \cdot \text{grad\_est}$
    \If{$\mathcal{M}(x'_{adv}) \neq y_{orig}$}
        \State \Return $x'_{adv}$ \Comment{Found a valid adversarial point}
    \EndIf
    \State $\epsilon \gets \epsilon \times 2$ \Comment{Increase step size and try again}
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subparagraph{Component 3: The "Jump" (Binary Search Projection)}
Finally, the algorithm projects the new point back onto the decision boundary. Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_jump} shows how this is done with a highly efficient binary search. It interpolates between the new adversarial point and the original clean image, rapidly converging on a point that lies almost exactly on the boundary, ready for the next iteration.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Binary Search Projection ("Jump")}
\label{alg:hsja_jump}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Adversarial point $x_{adv}$, Original point $x_{orig}$, Model $\mathcal{M}$
\Ensure A new adversarial point $x''_{adv}$ on the boundary
\State $y_{orig} \gets \mathcal{M}(x_{orig})$
\State $low \gets 0.0$, $high \gets 1.0$, $x''_{adv} \gets x_{adv}$
\For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} max\_trials}
    \State $mid \gets (low + high) / 2$
    \State $x_{cand} \gets (1 - mid) \cdot x_{orig} + mid \cdot x_{adv}$
    \If{$\mathcal{M}(x_{cand}) \neq y_{orig}$}
        \State $high \gets mid$; \quad $x''_{adv} \gets x_{cand}$
    \Else
        \State $low \gets mid$
    \EndIf
    \If{$high - low < \text{threshold}$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $x''_{adv}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

    In concert, these algorithms form a complete framework for executing the HopSkipJumpAttack. The initialization phase (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_init}) secures a valid starting point, after which the main loop (Algorithm~\ref{alg:hsja_main}) uses the 'Hop', 'Skip', and 'Jump' sub-routines to iteratively refine the adversarial example. The primary advantage of this multi-stage methodology is its ability to find minimally perturbed solutions with high query efficiency. Having detailed the algorithmic underpinnings of the attack, we now proceed to evaluate its practical performance against our trained model.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:detection_intro}

Object detection represents a significant leap in complexity beyond simple image classification. Instead of assigning a single label to an entire image, object detection aims to solve a dual problem: identifying \textit{what} objects are present in an image and precisely localizing \textit{where} they are located using \textbf{bounding boxes}. This capability for fine-grained scene understanding is critical for applications that require interaction with the physical world, most notably autonomous driving. In this context, the reliable detection of traffic signs, pedestrians, and other vehicles is a fundamental prerequisite for safe navigation.

To align our research with this critical application, our experiments are centered on the \textbf{LISA (Laboratory for Intelligent \& Safe Automobiles) Traffic Sign Dataset}. This dataset is specifically designed to benchmark the performance of object detectors on real-world traffic signs captured under a variety of conditions, making it an ideal environment to assess the practical security risks posed by adversarial attacks.

For our model, we have selected \textbf{YOLOv8n (You Only Look Once, version 8 nano)}. As a one-stage detector, the YOLO architecture is renowned for its exceptional balance of speed and accuracy, making it a leading choice for real-time applications where low-latency inference is paramount. The 'nano' variant, in particular, is optimized for efficiency, making it highly suitable for deployment on resource-constrained hardware, such as the on-board computers in autonomous vehicles. Our trained YOLOv8n model establishes a strong performance baseline on the clean LISA dataset, achieving a mean Average Precision (\textbf{mAP\textsubscript{50-95}}) of \textbf{81.1\%} and a \textbf{mAP\textsubscript{50}} of \textbf{92.7\%}. This strong detection performance, coupled with a rapid inference time of approximately \textbf{3.9 milliseconds} per image on our hardware, confirms its suitability for real-world deployment.

Given that such a high-performing and efficient model is a prime candidate for use in safety-critical systems, this chapter will investigate its robustness against adversarial attacks. We will explore methods designed to fool object detectors into failing to detect signs or misclassifying them, and subsequently evaluate defenses intended to mitigate these critical vulnerabilities.\section{Attacks}
\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
To assess the effectiveness of adversarial attacks, including the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), on the YOLO object detection model, a systematic evaluation framework was designed. The primary goal was to quantify the impact of perturbations on detection performance while ensuring the methodology is reproducible and aligned with standard practices in computer vision research.

The evaluation was conducted on a test dataset of traffic sign images, with a fixed confidence threshold of 0.25 to filter predictions. This threshold was selected to balance precision and recall, ensuring that only highly confident detections were considered while minimizing false positives. Performance was measured using the mean Average Precision (mAP) metric, with a focus on mAP@0.5, which evaluates detection accuracy at an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5. This metric is widely adopted in object detection benchmarks (e.g., Pascal VOC, COCO) and provides a practical measure of model robustness under moderate localization requirements. Additionally, mAP@0.5:0.95 was computed, averaging precision across IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, to assess the attack's impact on both detection and precise localization, offering a more stringent evaluation where relevant.

Class-specific mAP@0.5 was evaluated to identify vulnerabilities across different traffic sign categories (e.g., Speed Limit signs, Stop signs), with the drop in mAP ($\Delta$ mAP) calculated to highlight the relative degradation for each class. This approach enables a detailed understanding of how visual saliency and feature distinctiveness affect robustness. The methodology was applied consistently across all attacks to ensure comparability, with results presented in subsequent sections to avoid redundancy in attack-specific discussions.
\subsection{White-Box Attacks}
\subsubsection{FGSM Attack on YOLO}

\paragraph{Introduction}\leavevmode\\
The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) is a single-step adversarial attack that generates adversarial examples by perturbing inputs along the gradient of the loss function \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}. For YOLO \cite{lu2017noneed}, FGSM targets high-confidence detections from raw model outputs before non-maximum suppression (NMS) to maximize classification errors, causing misclassifications or missed detections. The attack uses an $\ell_\infty$-bounded perturbation (controlled by $\epsilon$) to maintain similarity while disrupting the model's predictions.

\paragraph{Methodology}\leavevmode\\

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) Attack on YOLO}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{\text{image}}$, $\text{model}$, $\epsilon$, $\text{conf\_threshold}$, $\text{device}$ \Comment{Input image, YOLO model, perturbation magnitude, confidence threshold, device}
\Ensure $x_{\text{adv}}$ \Comment{Adversarial image}
\State $x_{\text{input}} \gets \Call{preprocess\_image}{x_{\text{image}}}. \Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Preprocess and move to device}
\If{$x_{\text{input}}.\text{ndim} = 3$}
    \State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\Call{unsqueeze}{0}$ \Comment{Add batch dimension}
\EndIf
\State $x_{\text{input}}. \Call{requires\_grad\_}{}$ \Comment{Enable gradient tracking}
\State $raw\_outputs \gets \text{model}(x_{\text{input}})$ \Comment{Get raw predictions}
\State $raw\_preds \gets raw\_outputs[0]$ \Comment{Extract first detection head output}
\State $box\_coords \gets raw\_preds[..., :4]$ \Comment{Bounding box coordinates}
\State $obj\_scores \gets raw\_preds[..., 4:5]$ \Comment{Objectness scores}
\State $cls\_logits \gets raw\_preds[..., 5:]$ \Comment{Class logits}
\State $obj\_probs \gets \Call{sigmoid}{obj\_scores}$ \Comment{Convert objectness to probability}
\State $cls\_probs \gets \Call{softmax}{cls\_logits, \text{dim}=-1}.\Call{max}{\text{dim}=-1}$ \Comment{Max class probability}
\State $conf\_scores \gets obj\_probs \cdot cls\_probs$ \Comment{Combined confidence scores}
\State $mask \gets conf\_scores.\Call{squeeze}{-1} > \text{conf\_threshold}$ \Comment{Filter high-confidence detections}
\If{not $\Call{any}{mask}$}
    \State \Return None \Comment{No high-confidence detections}
\EndIf
\State $target\_classes \gets cls\_logits.\Call{argmax}{\text{dim}=-1}[mask].\Call{detach}{}$ \Comment{Original predicted classes}
\State $current\_logits \gets cls\_logits[mask]$ \Comment{Logits for high-confidence detections}
\State $loss \gets \Call{cross\_entropy}{current\_logits, target\_classes}$ \Comment{Classification loss}
\State $\text{model}.\Call{zero\_grad}{}$ \Comment{Clear gradients}
\State $loss.\Call{backward}{}$ \Comment{Compute gradients}
\State $grad \gets x_{\text{input}}.\text{grad}$ \Comment{Get image gradient}
\State $perturbation \gets \epsilon \cdot \Call{sign}{grad}$ \Comment{Compute perturbation}
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}} + perturbation$ \Comment{Generate adversarial image}
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets \Call{clamp}{x_{\text{adv}}, 0, 1}$ \Comment{Clamp to valid pixel range}
\State \Return $x_{\text{adv}}.\Call{detach}{}.\Call{cpu}{}$ \Comment{Return adversarial image}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsubsection{PGD Attack on YOLO}

% Writing introduction paragraph
\paragraph{Introduction}\leavevmode\\
The Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attack is an iterative adversarial attack that generates adversarial examples by repeatedly perturbing the input image along the gradient of the loss function, with projections to ensure perturbations remain within an $\ell_\infty$-bounded $\epsilon$-ball \cite{madry2018towardspgd}. For YOLO \cite{lu2017noneed}, PGD targets high-confidence detections from raw model outputs before non-maximum suppression (NMS), aiming to maximize classification errors and cause misclassifications or missed detections. Unlike the single-step FGSM, PGD's iterative nature with smaller step sizes and optional random initialization enhances attack strength while maintaining visual similarity, controlled by the perturbation magnitude $\epsilon$.

\newpage
% Writing methodology paragraph with algorithm
\paragraph{Methodology}\leavevmode\\
\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) Attack on YOLO}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{\text{image}}$, $\text{model}$, $\epsilon$, $num\_steps$, $\alpha$, $\text{conf\_threshold}$, $\text{random\_start}$, $\text{device}$ \Comment{Input image, YOLO model, perturbation magnitude, iterations, step size, confidence threshold, random start flag, device}
\Ensure $x_{\text{adv}}$ \Comment{Adversarial image}
\State $x_{\text{input}} \gets \Call{preprocess\_image}{x_{\text{image}}}. \Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Preprocess and move to device}
\If{$x_{\text{input}}.\text{ndim} = 3$}
    \State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\Call{unsqueeze}{0}$ \Comment{Add batch dimension}
\EndIf
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}}. \Call{clone}{}. \Call{detach}{}$ \Comment{Initialize adversarial image}
\If{$\text{random\_start}$}
    \State $noise \gets \Call{uniform}{-\epsilon, \epsilon, \text{shape}=x_{\text{adv}}.\text{shape}}$ \Comment{Random noise in $\epsilon$-ball}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{adv}} + noise$
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets \Call{clamp}{x_{\text{adv}}, 0, 1}$ \Comment{Clamp to valid pixel range}
\EndIf
\For{$i = 1$ to $num\_steps$}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}}. \Call{requires\_grad\_}{}$ \Comment{Enable gradient tracking}
    \State $raw\_outputs \gets \text{model}(x_{\text{adv}})$ \Comment{Get raw predictions}
    \State $raw\_preds \gets raw\_outputs[0]$ \Comment{Extract first detection head output}
    \State $box\_coords \gets raw\_preds[..., :4]$ \Comment{Bounding box coordinates}
    \State $obj\_scores \gets raw\_preds[..., 4:5]$ \Comment{Objectness scores}
    \State $cls\_logits \gets raw\_preds[..., 5:]$ \Comment{Class logits}
    \State $obj\_probs \gets \Call{sigmoid}{obj\_scores}$ \Comment{Convert objectness to probability}
    \State $cls\_probs \gets \Call{softmax}{cls\_logits, \text{dim}=-1}.\Call{max}{\text{dim}=-1}$ \Comment{Max class probability}
    \State $conf\_scores \gets obj\_probs \cdot cls\_probs$ \Comment{Combined confidence scores}
    \State $mask \gets conf\_scores.\Call{squeeze}{-1} > \text{conf\_threshold}$ \Comment{Filter high-confidence detections}
    \If{not $\Call{any}{mask}$}
        \State \Return None \Comment{No high-confidence detections}
    \EndIf
    \State $target\_classes \gets cls\_logits.\Call{argmax}{\text{dim}=-1}[mask].\Call{detach}{}$ \Comment{Original predicted classes}
    \State $current\_logits \gets cls\_logits[mask]$ \Comment{Logits for high-confidence detections}
    \State $loss \gets \Call{cross\_entropy}{current\_logits, target\_classes}$ \Comment{Classification loss}
    \State $\text{model}.\Call{zero\_grad}{}$ \Comment{Clear gradients}
    \State $loss.\Call{backward}{}$ \Comment{Compute gradients}
    \State $grad \gets x_{\text{adv}}.\text{grad}$ \Comment{Get image gradient}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{adv}} + \alpha \cdot \Call{sign}{grad}$ \Comment{Update adversarial image}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets \Call{clamp}{x_{\text{adv}}, x_{\text{input}} - \epsilon, x_{\text{input}} + \epsilon}$ \Comment{Project to $\epsilon$-ball}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets \Call{clamp}{x_{\text{adv}}, 0, 1}$ \Comment{Clamp to valid pixel range}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{adv}}.\Call{detach}{}$ \Comment{Detach for next iteration}
\EndFor
\State \Return $x_{\text{adv}}.\Call{detach}{}.\Call{cpu}{}$ \Comment{Return adversarial image}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\part{Language Models}
Human intelligence is inextricably linked to our ability to produce, understand, and reason with language. In the field of artificial intelligence, the quest to imbue machines with similar capabilities has culminated in the development of \textbf{Language Models}. At its core, a language model is a probabilistic model over sequences of linguistic units, such as words or tokens. While this concept began with simple statistical methods, it has evolved into the sophisticated deep learning architectures that now form the backbone of modern AI. This Part of the thesis provides a comprehensive investigation into the adversarial vulnerabilities of these models across their primary modalities and applications.

Our exploration begins at the interface between the physical world and symbolic text: \textbf{Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR)}. We treat ASR not merely as an audio processing task, but as the foundational gateway for machines to process spoken language. The systems that transcribe human speech into text are, in essence, sequence-to-sequence language models that must interpret continuous acoustic signals. Their security is the first line of defense for any downstream language-based application.

Once speech is transcribed, or when text is the native input, we enter the domain of \textbf{Traditional Natural Language Processing (NLP)}. This chapter will examine task-specific models designed for core applications like sentiment analysis and text classification. These systems, often built on architectures like Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) or early Transformers, represent a critical class of language models that are widely deployed for specific, well-defined problems.

The final stage of our investigation addresses the current paradigm shift in the field: \textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs)}. These vast, pre-trained models have redefined the scope of what is possible, demonstrating remarkable emergent abilities in reasoning, generation, and few-shot learning. By examining LLMs, we address the security challenges at the cutting edge of AI, where the attack surface is broader and the potential consequences of failure are more complex than ever before.

Despite their diverse applications and varying levels of complexity—from processing audio waves to generating coherent essays—these systems all share a profound and unsettling characteristic: a vulnerability to adversarial attacks. Whether through subtle, imperceptible noise in an audio waveform or carefully chosen word substitutions in a block of text, adversaries can manipulate the inputs to these models to cause spectacular failures. This Part, therefore, provides a holistic examination of the adversarial landscape for language-centric AI, charting the evolution of threats from the specialized models of yesterday to the general-purpose intelligent systems of today.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:asr_intro}

Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) is the technology that converts spoken language into machine-readable text, serving as the fundamental bridge between human speech and digital computation. It powers a vast array of modern applications, from ubiquitous voice assistants and hands-free device control to automated transcription services and security-sensitive systems like voice biometrics. As ASR becomes more deeply integrated into our daily lives, ensuring its reliability and security is of paramount importance. An attack that can maliciously alter a transcribed command or conversation poses a significant threat.

The landscape of ASR was dramatically reshaped by self-supervised learning models, chief among them \textbf{Wav2Vec 2.0}. Unlike traditional pipelines that require vast amounts of transcribed audio for training, Wav2Vec 2.0 learns powerful and context-rich representations of speech directly from raw, unlabeled audio waveforms. This ability has enabled it to achieve state-of-the-art performance on speech recognition benchmarks. For our investigation, we use a pre-trained Wav2Vec 2.0 model, making our analysis representative of a powerful, real-world system that an adversary would likely target.

To conduct a rigorous and reproducible security evaluation, our experiments are performed on a representative subset of \textbf{100 samples} drawn from the \textbf{LibriSpeech test-clean dataset}. LibriSpeech is a widely-recognized benchmark for ASR research, and by focusing on a specific subset, we can conduct a controlled and computationally manageable investigation into the effects of adversarial perturbations. This methodology ensures that our findings are grounded in a standard, high-quality corpus while allowing for a focused analysis of attack efficacy.

In this chapter, we will therefore probe the vulnerabilities of the pre-trained Wav2Vec 2.0 model against this well-defined testbed. On the 100 clean samples, our model establishes a strong performance baseline, achieving a \textbf{Character Error Rate (CER) of 0.9\%} and a \textbf{Word Error Rate (WER) of 2.84\%}. These low error rates confirm the model's high competence before the introduction of any malicious noise. The subsequent sections will detail the attack methodologies used to generate adversarial audio and explore potential defenses aimed at fortifying these advanced speech recognition systems.

\subsubsection{FGSM Attack}
\paragraph{Introduction}
The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) is a seminal single-step adversarial attack technique designed to generate adversarial examples by introducing imperceptible perturbations to input data, thereby misleading machine learning models \cite{goodfellow2015explaining}. In the context of automatic speech recognition (ASR), this attack crafts adversarial audio samples that force the model to produce a specified target transcription\cite{zelasko2021adversarial}.

\paragraph{Methodology}
\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) Attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{\text{audio}}$, $t_{\text{target}}$, $\text{processor}$, $\text{model}$, $\epsilon$, $sr$, $\text{device}$ \Comment{Input audio signal, target transcription, processor, model, perturbation magnitude, sample rate, device}
\Ensure $x_{\text{adv}}$ \Comment{Adversarial audio signal}
\State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{audio}}.\Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Move audio to device}
\If{$x_{\text{input}}.\text{ndim} = 1$}
    \State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\Call{unsqueeze}{0}$ \Comment{Add batch dimension}
\EndIf
\State $labels \gets \text{processor}.\text{tokenizer}(t_{\text{target}}, \text{return\_tensors}=\text{``pt''}).\text{input\_ids}.\Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Tokenize target}
\State $labels \gets labels.\Call{repeat}{x_{\text{input}}.\text{shape}[0], 1}$ \Comment{Replicate for batch}
\State $x_{\text{input}}.\Call{requires\_grad\_}{}$ \Comment{Enable gradient tracking}
\State $output \gets \text{model}(x_{\text{input}}, \text{labels}=labels)$ \Comment{Compute model output}
\State $loss \gets output.\text{loss}$ \Comment{Extract CTC loss}
\State $loss.\Call{backward}{}$ \Comment{Compute gradients}
\State $grad \gets x_{\text{input}}.\text{grad}$ \Comment{Get gradients}
\State $perturbation \gets \epsilon \cdot \Call{sign}{grad}$ \Comment{Compute perturbation}
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}} + perturbation$ \Comment{Generate adversarial input}
\State \Return $x_{\text{adv}}.\Call{detach}{}.\Call{cpu}{}$ \Comment{Return adversarial audio}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsubsection{PGD Attack}
\paragraph{Introduction}
The Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attack is a powerful iterative adversarial attack method that builds upon the principles of the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) by applying multiple small perturbations to optimize the adversarial example within an $\ell_\infty$ norm constraint \cite{madry2018towardspgd}. In automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems, PGD generates adversarial audio samples by iteratively adjusting the input waveform to maximize the loss function, aiming to force the model to produce a specified target transcription \cite{zelasko2021adversarial}.


\paragraph{Methodology}
\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) Attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{\text{audio}}$, $t_{\text{target}}$, $\text{model}$, $\text{processor}$, $\epsilon$, $\alpha$, $num\_iter$, $sr$, $\text{device}$, $\delta$ \Comment{Input audio, target transcription, model, processor, perturbation bound, step size, iterations, sample rate, device, convergence threshold}
\Ensure $x_{\text{adv}}$ \Comment{Adversarial audio signal}
\State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{audio}}.\Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Move audio to device}
\If{$x_{\text{input}}.\text{ndim} = 1$}
    \State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\Call{unsqueeze}{0}$ \Comment{Add batch dimension}
\EndIf
\State $labels \gets \text{processor}.\text{tokenizer}(t_{\text{target}}, \text{return\_tensors}=\text{``pt''}).\text{input\_ids}.\Call{to}{\text{device}}$ \Comment{Tokenize target}
\State $labels \gets labels.\Call{repeat}{x_{\text{input}}.\text{shape}[0], 1}$ \Comment{Replicate for batch}
\State $perturbation \gets \Call{zeros\_like}{x_{\text{input}}, \text{requires\_grad}=\text{True}, \text{device}=\text{device}}$ \Comment{Initialize perturbation}
\State $prev\_loss \gets \infty$ \Comment{Initialize previous loss}
\For{$i \gets 1$ to $num\_iter$} \Comment{Iterate up to max iterations}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}} + perturbation$ \Comment{Compute adversarial input}
    \State $output \gets \text{model}(x_{\text{adv}}, \text{labels}=labels)$ \Comment{Compute model output}
    \State $loss \gets output.\text{loss}$ \Comment{Extract CTC loss}
    \If{$i > 1$ and $|prev\_loss - loss| < \delta$} \Comment{Check convergence}
        \State \textbf{break} \Comment{Stop if loss change is below threshold}
    \EndIf
    \State $prev\_loss \gets loss$ \Comment{Update previous loss}
    \State $\text{model}.\Call{zero\_grad}{}$ \Comment{Clear gradients}
    \State $loss.\Call{backward}{}$ \Comment{Compute gradients}
    \State \Comment{Update perturbation without tracking gradients}
    \State \Comment{Begin mixed precision block}
    \State $perturbation \gets perturbation - \alpha \cdot \Call{sign}{perturbation.\text{grad}}$ \Comment{Update perturbation}
    \State $perturbation \gets \Call{clamp}{perturbation, -\epsilon, \epsilon}$ \Comment{Project to $\ell_\infty$ ball}
    \State \Comment{End mixed precision block}
    \State $perturbation.\text{grad}.\Call{zero\_}{}$ \Comment{Reset gradient}
\EndFor
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}} + perturbation$ \Comment{Final adversarial input}
\State \Return $x_{\text{adv}}.\Call{detach}{}.\Call{cpu}{}$ \Comment{Return adversarial audio}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsubsection{Cramér-IPM Attack}
\paragraph{Introduction}
The Cramér Integral Probability Metric (IPM) attack is an advanced adversarial method designed to generate robust adversarial audio signals for automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems,\cite{esmaeilpour2021towards}. By leveraging the Cramér-IPM, this attack minimizes the discrepancy between the distributions of original and adversarial audio samples, ensuring the adversarial signals remain close to the legitimate speech manifold. Unlike traditional attacks like FGSM or PGD, the Cramér-IPM approach combines the Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) loss with a statistical distance metric, enhancing robustness against over-the-air playback without relying on costly Expectation Over Transformation (EOT) operations \cite{esmaeilpour2021towards}. This method optimizes perturbations to maximize transcription errors while maintaining perceptual similarity, making it effective for both targeted and non-targeted attacks.

\paragraph{Methodology}

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Cramér-IPM Adversarial Attack}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{\text{audio}}$, $t_{\text{target}}$, $\text{model}$, $\text{processor}$, $\epsilon$, $sr$, $\text{num\_iter}$, $\lambda_{\text{ipm}}$, $n_{\text{samples}}$, $\text{device}$ \Comment{Input audio, target transcription, model, processor, perturbation bound, sample rate, iterations, IPM weight, sample points, device}
\Ensure $x_{\text{adv}}$ \Comment{Adversarial audio signal}

\State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{audio}}.\text{to}(\text{device})$ \Comment{Move audio to device}
\If{$x_{\text{input}}.\text{ndim} = 1$}
    \State $x_{\text{input}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\text{unsqueeze}(0)$ \Comment{Add batch dimension}
\EndIf

\State $labels \gets \text{processor}.\text{tokenizer}(t_{\text{target}}, \text{return\_tensors}=\text{``pt''}).\text{input\_ids}.\text{to}(\text{device})$ \Comment{Tokenize target}
\State $labels \gets labels.\text{repeat}(x_{\text{input}}.\text{shape}[0], 1)$ \Comment{Replicate for batch}
\State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{input}}.\text{clone}().\text{detach}().\text{requires\_grad\_}(\text{True})$ \Comment{Initialize adversarial input}

\For{$i \gets 1$ to $\text{num\_iter}$} \Comment{Iterate up to max iterations}
    \State $adv_{\text{iter}} \gets x_{\text{adv}}.\text{clone}().\text{detach}().\text{requires\_grad\_}(\text{True})$ \Comment{Copy for iteration}
    \State $\text{model}.\text{zero\_grad}()$ \Comment{Clear gradients}
    \State $output \gets \text{model}(adv_{\text{iter}}, \text{labels}=labels)$ \Comment{Compute model output}
    \State $loss_{\text{ctc}} \gets output.\text{loss}$ \Comment{Extract CTC loss}
    \State $loss_{\text{ctc}}.\text{backward}()$ \Comment{Compute CTC gradients}
    \State $grad_{\text{ctc}} \gets adv_{\text{iter}}.\text{grad}.\text{clone}()$ \Comment{Store CTC gradient}
    
    \State \Comment{Compute IPM without gradients}
    \State $ipm_{\text{val}} \gets \text{compute\_cramer\_ipm}(x_{\text{input}}, adv_{\text{iter}}.\text{detach}(), n_{\text{samples}})$ \Comment{Compute IPM}
    \State $adv_{\text{ipm}} \gets x_{\text{adv}}.\text{clone}().\text{detach}().\text{requires\_grad\_}(\text{True})$ \Comment{Copy for IPM gradient}
    \State $perturb \gets \text{randn\_like}(adv_{\text{ipm}}) \cdot 10^{-5}$ \Comment{Small random perturbation}
    \State $adv_{\text{perturbed}} \gets adv_{\text{ipm}} + perturb$ \Comment{Perturbed input}
    \State $ipm_{\text{val}} \gets \text{compute\_cramer\_ipm}(x_{\text{input}}, adv_{\text{ipm}}, n_{\text{samples}})$ \Comment{Base IPM}
    \State $ipm_{\text{perturbed}} \gets \text{compute\_cramer\_ipm}(x_{\text{input}}, adv_{\text{perturbed}}, n_{\text{samples}})$ \Comment{Perturbed IPM}
    \State $grad_{\text{ipm}} \gets \frac{ipm_{\text{perturbed}} - ipm_{\text{val}}}{10^{-5}} \cdot \text{mean}(perturb)$ \Comment{Finite difference gradient}
    \State $grad_{\text{combined}} \gets grad_{\text{ctc}} - \lambda_{\text{ipm}} \cdot grad_{\text{ipm}}$ \Comment{Combine gradients}
    \State $perturbation \gets \epsilon \cdot \text{sign}(grad_{\text{combined}})$ \Comment{Generate perturbation}
    
    \State \Comment{Update adversarial input}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets x_{\text{adv}} - perturbation$ \Comment{Apply perturbation}
    \State $x_{\text{adv}} \gets \text{clamp}(x_{\text{adv}}, -1.0, 1.0)$ \Comment{Clamp to valid range}
\EndFor

\State \Return $x_{\text{adv}}.\text{detach}().\text{cpu}()$ \Comment{Return adversarial audio}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{Randomized Smoothing}
\subsubsection{Introduction}
Randomized smoothing \cite{cohen2019certified} emerges as a promising defense by creating noise-invariant predictions. And it is an effective defense with speech to text models\cite{zelasko2021adversarial}. We implemented it with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive variance scaling \cite{li2024adaptive}:} This technique adjusts the noise level (variance) added to an input signal based on its local characteristics, such as signal variance. In speech recognition, it dynamically scales the noise to be lower in high-variance regions (to preserve speech content) and higher in low-variance regions (to mask potential adversarial perturbations), enhancing robustness while maintaining accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Batch Processing for Efficient Monte Carlo Sampling \cite{cohen2019certified}:} This approach improves the efficiency of randomized smoothing by processing multiple noisy versions of an input signal in batches. Instead of evaluating each perturbed sample individually, batch processing leverages parallel computation to perform Monte Carlo sampling, estimating the smoothed classifier's prediction faster and more scalably. 
    \item \textbf{ROVER voting \cite{olivier2022sequential}:} ROVER (Recognizer Output Voting Error Reduction) is a method for combining multiple transcription outputs from a speech recognition system. It aggregates transcriptions by voting on words at each position across noisy samples, selecting the most frequent word to produce a final, more robust transcription, reducing errors caused by noise or adversarial attacks.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Methodology}


\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Randomized Smoothing Defense}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{audio}$, processor, model, $\sigma_{base}$, $num_samples$ \Comment{Input audio signal, processor, model, base noise level, number of samples}
\Ensure $transcription$, $\sigma_{used}$ \Comment{Defended transcription, used noise level}

\State $\sigma \gets adaptive\_sigma(x_{audio}, \sigma_{base})$ \Comment{Compute adaptive noise level}
\State $noisy\_audios \gets [add\_gaussian\_noise(x_{audio}, \sigma) \text{ for } i \text{ in } 1 \text{ to } num_samples]$ \Comment{Generate noisy samples}
\State $transcriptions \gets []$ \Comment{Initialize transcription list}
\For{$i \gets 1$ to $num_samples$ step $batch\_size$} \Comment{Process in batches}
    \State $batch \gets noisy\_audios[i:i+batch\_size]$
    \State $inputs \gets processor(batch, sampling\_rate=16000, return\_tensors="pt", padding="longest").input\_values.to(model.device)$ \Comment{Prepare inputs}
    \State $logits \gets model(inputs).logits$ \Comment{Compute logits}
    \State $pred\_ids \gets \arg\max(logits, dim=-1)$ \Comment{Get predicted IDs}
    \State $batch\_transcriptions \gets processor.batch\_decode(pred\_ids)$ \Comment{Decode predictions}
    \State $transcriptions \gets transcriptions \cup batch\_transcriptions$ \Comment{Add to transcription list}
\EndFor
\State $word\_votes \gets dictionary()$ \Comment{Initialize vote counter}
\For{$ts \in transcriptions$} \Comment{Count word votes}
    \State $words \gets split(ts)$
    \For{$idx, word \in enumerate(words)$}
        \State $word\_votes[idx][word] \gets word\_votes[idx][word] + 1$
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\State $final\_transcription \gets []$ \Comment{Initialize final transcription}
\For{$idx \in sorted(keys(word\_votes))$} \Comment{Construct final transcription}
    \If{$idx \in word\_votes$ and $word\_votes[idx]$}
        \State $final\_word \gets word \text{ with } \max(word\_votes[idx][word])$
        \State $final\_transcription \gets final\_transcription \cup \{final\_word\}$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $transcription \gets " ".join(final\_transcription)$ \Comment{Join words into transcription}
\State \Return $transcription, \sigma$ \Comment{Return transcription and used sigma}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{MP3 Compression}
\subsubsection{Introduction}
MP3 compression, a lossy audio encoding scheme based on psychoacoustic models, serves as an effective defense against Audio Adversarial Examples (AAEs) in Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems \cite{andronic2020mp3}. By discarding audio information below human hearing thresholds, MP3 compression can reduce adversarial noise (AN) embedded in AAEs, which is often imperceptible to humans but misleads ASR systems into incorrect transcriptions \cite{andronic2020mp3}. We implement this defense with the following key components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MP3 Compression and Decompression \cite{andronic2020mp3}:} The audio signal is compressed to MP3 format at varying bitrates (e.g., 128 kbps to 4 kbps) and then decompressed back to the time domain. This process removes frequency components where AN is typically hidden, improving transcription accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Bitrate Variation:} Different bitrates are tested to evaluate the trade-off between noise reduction and audio quality preservation. Lower bitrates increase noise reduction but may degrade speech content \cite{andronic2020mp3}.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation Metrics:} Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) are used to measure the defense's effectiveness against Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks, with Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) improvements confirming AN reduction \cite{andronic2020mp3}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Methodology}
\begin{breakablealgorithm}{MP3 Compression Defense}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x_{audio}$, processor, model, $bitrate$ \Comment{Input audio signal, processor, model, MP3 bitrate}
\Ensure $transcription$, $bitrate_{used}$ \Comment{Defended transcription, used bitrate}

\State $audio_{float32} \gets convert\_to\_float32(x_{audio})$ \Comment{Convert to float32}
\State $audio_{float32} \gets clip(audio_{float32}, -1.0, 1.0)$ \Comment{Clip to [-1, 1]}
\State $audio_{int16} \gets (audio_{float32} \cdot 32767) \text{ as } int16$ \Comment{Convert to 16-bit PCM}
\If{$audio_{int16}$ has multiple channels}
    \State $audio_{int16} \gets squeeze(audio_{int16})$ \Comment{Flatten to mono}
\EndIf
\State $audio_{segment} \gets AudioSegment(audio_{int16}, sample\_rate=16000, sample\_width=2, channels=1)$ \Comment{Create audio segment}
\State $mp3\_buffer \gets create\_memory\_buffer()$ \Comment{Create in-memory buffer}
\State $export(audio_{segment}, mp3\_buffer, format="mp3", bitrate=bitrate)$ \Comment{Export to memory as MP3}
\State $compressed_{audio} \gets import\_audio(mp3\_buffer, format="mp3")$ \Comment{Read from memory}
\State $close(mp3\_buffer)$ \Comment{Clean up buffer}
\State $audio_{samples} \gets get\_samples(compressed_{audio}, dtype=float32)$ \Comment{Convert to numpy array}
\State $audio_{normalized} \gets audio_{samples} / 32767$ \Comment{Normalize to [-1, 1]}
\If{$length(audio_{normalized}) \neq length(x_{audio})$}
    \If{$length(audio_{normalized}) > length(x_{audio})$}
        \State $audio_{normalized} \gets audio_{normalized}[:length(x_{audio})]$ \Comment{Trim to match input}
    \Else
        \State $audio_{normalized} \gets concatenate([audio_{normalized}, zeros(length(x_{audio}) - length(audio_{normalized}))])$ \Comment{Pad with zeros}
    \EndIf
\EndIf
\State $transcription \gets transcribe\_audio(audio_{normalized}, 16000, processor, model)$ \Comment{Transcribe defended audio}
\State \Return $transcription, bitrate$ \Comment{Return transcription and used bitrate}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{Spectral Gating Defense}
\subsubsection{Introduction}
Spectral gating is a defense strategy that applies noise reduction to audio signals to remove adversarial perturbations\cite{oreilly2022voiceblock}.
The spectral gating defense processes a 1D audio signal as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Voice Activity Detection}: Apply WebRTC VAD to generate a boolean mask identifying speech segments, using a frame duration of 30 ms and an aggressiveness level of 2.
    \item \textbf{Noise Profile Extraction}: Extract non-speech segments based on the VAD mask. If no non-speech segments exist, use portions from the audio's beginning and end (0.5 seconds each).
    \item \textbf{Noise Reduction}: Use the \texttt{noisereduce} library to perform spectral subtraction, configured with parameters for stationary noise, proportion of noise to remove, and frequency/time smoothing.
    \item \textbf{Output Adjustment}: Ensure the output audio matches the input length by trimming or padding as needed, and cast to float32 format.
\end{enumerate}


\subsubsection{Methodology}

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Spectral Gating Defense}
\label{alg:spectral_gating}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Audio signal $x$ (float32 or convertible, [-1,1]), sample rate $sr$, stationary flag $s$, proportion decrease $p$, frequency smoothing $f_{hz}$, time smoothing $t_{ms}$
\Ensure Denoised audio signal $y$ (float32, same length as $x$)
\State $x \gets x.\text{astype}(\text{float32})$ \Comment{Convert to float32}
\State $x \gets \text{Clip}(x, -1.0, 1.0)$ \Comment{Ensure signal is in [-1,1]}
\State $x \gets \text{Squeeze}(x)$ \Comment{Remove singleton dimensions}
\State $m \gets \text{VoiceActivityDetection}(x, sr)$ \Comment{Generate VAD mask}
\State $n \gets \text{ExtractNoiseProfile}(x, m, sr)$ \Comment{Get noise from non-speech, fallback to edges if none}
\State $y \gets \text{ReduceNoise}(x, sr, n, s, p, f_{hz}, t_{ms})$ \Comment{Apply spectral gating}
\If{$\text{length}(y) > \text{length}(x)$}
    \State $y \gets y[0:\text{length}(x)]$ \Comment{Trim excess from start}
\ElsIf{$\text{length}(y) < \text{length}(x)$}
    \State $y \gets \text{Pad}(y, \text{length}(x) - \text{length}(y), \text{mode="constant"})$ \Comment{Pad with zeros}
\EndIf
\State \Return $y.\text{astype}(\text{float32})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}

\subsection{Quantization Defense}
\subsubsection{Introduction}
Input quantization reduces the precision of audio signals by mapping continuous values to discrete levels, effectively attenuating adversarial perturbations \cite{li2024investigating}. The defense preprocesses audio inputs by quantizing them to a specified bit-width (e.g., 4-bit, 8-bit, 16-bit), scaling values to a discrete range, and dequantizing back to the original scale. This process introduces noise that can disrupt adversarial perturbations while preserving speech intelligibility.

\subsubsection{Methodology}
The input quantization defense processes a 1D audio signal as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clamping}: Ensure the audio signal is within the range $[-1, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Scaling}: Map the signal to $[0, 2^b - 1]$, where $b$ is the bit-width.
    \item \textbf{Quantization}: Round the scaled values to the nearest integer.
    \item \textbf{Dequantization}: Scale the quantized values back to $[-1, 1]$.
    \item \textbf{Output}: Return the dequantized signal as a float32 array.
\end{enumerate}

The pseudocode for the input quantization defense is shown in Algorithm \ref{alg:input_quantization}.

\begin{breakablealgorithm}{Input Quantization Defense}
\label{alg:input_quantization}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Audio signal $x$ (float32, [-1,1]), bit-width $b$, device $d$
\Ensure Quantized audio signal $y$ (float32, same shape as $x$)
\State $x \gets \text{Clamp}(x, -1.0, 1.0)$ \Comment{Ensure signal is in [-1,1]}
\State $x \gets \text{ToTensor}(x, d)$ \Comment{Convert to tensor on device}
\State $n \gets 2^b$ \Comment{Number of quantization levels}
\State $s \gets (x + 1) / 2 \times (n - 1)$ \Comment{Scale to [0, n-1]}
\State $q \gets \text{Round}(s)$ \Comment{Quantize to integers}
\State $y \gets (q / (n - 1)) \times 2 - 1$ \Comment{Dequantize to [-1,1]}
\State \Return $y.\text{cpu}().\text{numpy}().\text{astype}(\text{float32})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{breakablealgorithm}
